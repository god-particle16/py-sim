Spring and Mass (SHM)

What you are seeing

When you stretch or compress a spring, it pulls or pushes back with a force proportional to the displacement. This is Hooke's law. A force that always points towards the equilibrium position and grows with distance produces simple harmonic motion (SHM): a smooth, repeating oscillation described by a sine wave.

Energy flows back and forth. At the ends of the motion the block is momentarily at rest and all the energy is stored in the spring. At the centre the spring is relaxed and all the energy is kinetic. Without damping, the total never changes.

Key formulas

F = -k x (Hooke's law)
ω = √(k / m)
T = 2π √(m / k)
x(t) = A cos(ω t)
E = ½ k A² = ½ m v² + ½ k x²

With damping, a friction-like force -b v removes energy and the amplitude shrinks exponentially. If damping is very strong the block creeps back without oscillating at all (overdamped).

Try this

  1. Set damping to 0. Watch the energy bars: do the kinetic and potential bars ever add up to more than the total?
  2. Double the mass. How does the period change? Predict first, then check.
  3. Make the spring four times stiffer. What happens to the period?
  4. Release the block from a larger and a smaller displacement. Does the period change?
  5. Raise damping above 2.5 and see the motion become overdamped.

Common mistakes

  • Believing the period depends on amplitude. For an ideal spring it does not.
  • Forgetting the minus sign in Hooke's law. The force opposes the displacement.
  • Thinking the velocity is greatest at the ends. It is greatest at the centre.

Check yourself

1. A 0.5 kg mass on a 50 N/m spring. What is the period?

T = 2π√(0.5/50) = 2π(0.1) ≈ 0.63 s.

2. Where is the acceleration zero?

At the equilibrium position, because the spring force is zero there.

3. By what factor does the maximum speed change if the amplitude doubles?

It doubles, because vmax = Aω and ω stays the same.
