Gas Laws

What you are seeing

A gas is a huge number of tiny particles moving in random directions and bouncing off the walls. The pressure you measure is the average force per unit area of all those collisions. Raise the temperature and the particles move faster, so they hit the walls harder and more often: pressure goes up. Squeeze the container and the same particles hit a smaller wall area more frequently: pressure goes up. Add more particles and there are simply more collisions.

For a fixed amount of gas these relationships are summed up in three named laws, and all three are special cases of the ideal gas law.

Key formulas

Ideal gas law: P V = n R T = N kB T
Boyle's law (T, n constant): P V = constant
Charles's law (P, n constant): V / T = constant
Gay-Lussac's law (V, n constant): P / T = constant
Average kinetic energy per particle: &frac32; kB T

Temperature must always be in kelvin. The simulation is a 2D version of the real thing, so its pressure is a force per unit length, shown in arbitrary but consistent units.

Try this

  1. Hold temperature constant. Halve the volume. Does the pressure double (Boyle's law)?
  2. Hold volume constant. Double the temperature. What happens to the pressure and the average speed?
  3. Hold pressure constant and raise the temperature. What does the wall do?
  4. Double the number of particles at fixed T and V. What happens to the pressure?
  5. Check the P × V readout against the number of particles times the temperature.

Common mistakes

  • Using Celsius in gas-law calculations. Always convert to kelvin.
  • Thinking pressure comes from particles pushing each other. It comes from collisions with the container walls.
  • Believing all particles move at the same speed. They have a spread of speeds; only the average depends on T.

Check yourself

1. A gas at 2 atm is compressed to half its volume at constant temperature. What is the new pressure?

By Boyle's law, P2 = 2 × 2 = 4 atm.

2. A gas at 300 K is heated to 600 K at constant volume. What happens to the pressure?

It doubles, because P is proportional to T at constant V.

3. Why does a sealed can of air explode if heated strongly?

The volume is fixed, so rising temperature raises the pressure until the can cannot withstand it.
