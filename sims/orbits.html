Gravity and Orbits

What you are seeing

Gravity pulls the planet towards the star. If the planet were dropped from rest it would fall straight in. Because it is launched sideways, it keeps missing: it falls towards the star, but its sideways motion carries it around. An orbit is simply a continuous fall that never hits the ground. Launch slowly and the planet swings close to the star on a tight ellipse. Launch at exactly the circular speed and the orbit is a circle. Launch faster and it climbs to a far point before falling back. Above the escape speed it never returns.

Key formulas

F = G M m / r²
Circular orbit speed: v = √(G M / r)
Escape speed: vesc = √2 × vcircular ≈ 1.41 vcircular
Kepler's 3rd law: T² = (4π² / G M) a³ (T in years, a in AU, M in Suns: T² = a³ / M)

Kepler's laws. (1) Orbits are ellipses with the star at one focus. (2) A line from the star to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, so the planet moves faster when it is closer. (3) The square of the period is proportional to the cube of the average distance.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a planet in orbit feels no gravity. It is constantly accelerating towards the star.
  • Believing the planet moves at constant speed. On an ellipse it speeds up near the star and slows down far away.
  • Imagining that a spacecraft has to "escape gravity". It only needs enough speed to coast away, since gravity never fully vanishes.

Check yourself

1. A planet is 4 AU from a 1-Sun star on a circular orbit. What is its period?

T² = a³ = 64, so T = 8 years.

2. Where on an elliptical orbit is the planet fastest?

At the closest point to the star (perihelion), because angular momentum is conserved and the radius is smallest.

3. Why do satellites in lower orbits go around faster?

Gravity is stronger closer in, so a higher speed is needed to stay on a circular path there.
