What you are seeing

A simple pendulum is a small mass hanging from a light string. When you pull it aside, gravity gives it a restoring force that always points back towards the lowest point. The bob speeds up as it falls, overshoots the bottom, slows down on the other side and repeats. The time for one full back-and-forth swing is the period.

Galileo noticed that a swinging chandelier took the same time for big and small swings. This is almost true for small angles, and this simulation lets you find out exactly how far the idea works.

Key formulas

T = 2π √(L / g) (small angles, less than about 15°)
f = 1 / T
θ'' = -(g / L) sin θ (full equation of motion)

The mass of the bob does not appear in the formula. Gravity pulls harder on a heavy bob, but a heavy bob also has more inertia, and the two effects cancel.

  1. Set the angle to 10° and damping to 0. Compare the measured and small-angle periods.
  2. Increase the angle to 90° and then 150°. What happens to the measured period?
  3. Quadruple the length from 0.5 m to 2.0 m. By what factor does the period change?
  4. Move the pendulum to the Moon. How does the period compare with Earth?

Common mistakes

  • Using the formula T = 2π√(L/g) for large swings. It is only accurate for small angles; the real period grows with amplitude.
  • Measuring the length to the top of the bob. It should be measured to the centre of the bob.
  • Thinking a heavier bob swings faster. Mass cancels out.

Check yourself

1. What is the period of a 1.0 m pendulum on Earth?

T = 2π√(1/9.81) ≈ 2.0 s.

2. To double the period, what must you do to the length?

Quadruple it. T is proportional to √L, so doubling T needs four times the length.

3. Where is the bob moving fastest, and why?

At the lowest point, where all the gravitational potential energy has turned into kinetic energy.
