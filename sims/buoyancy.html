What you are seeing

Any object placed in a fluid pushes some fluid out of the way. The fluid pushes back with an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced. This is Archimedes' principle. If the buoyant force is bigger than the object's weight, the object rises; if it is smaller, the object sinks. A floating object settles at the depth where the displaced fluid weighs exactly the same as the object.

This works out neatly in terms of density. An object that is less dense than the liquid floats, with a submerged fraction equal to the density ratio. An object that is denser than the liquid sinks. Ships made of steel float because their hollow shape displaces a large volume of water compared with their mass.

Key formulas

Buoyant force: Fb = ρfluid Vsubmerged g
Weight: W = ρobject V g
Floating: Fb = W ⇒ submerged fraction = ρobject / ρfluid
Sinks if ρobject > ρfluid

Common mistakes

  • Believing heavy objects always sink. What matters is density, not weight: an iron ship floats, a small pebble sinks.
  • Thinking buoyant force depends on the object's material. It depends only on the volume of fluid displaced and the fluid's density.
  • Forgetting that a fully submerged object has a fixed buoyant force; going deeper does not change it.

Check yourself

1. A wooden block of density 700 kg/m³ floats in water. What fraction is under water?

700 / 1000 = 0.70, so 70 percent.

2. A 0.008 m³ block is fully submerged in water. What is the buoyant force?

F = 1000 × 0.008 × 9.81 ≈ 78 N.

3. Why is it easier to float in sea water than in a freshwater lake?

Sea water is denser, so less of your body has to be submerged to displace a weight of water equal to your own weight.
